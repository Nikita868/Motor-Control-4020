Touch in action

Why touch matters for movement

We lean on cutaneous feedback constantly while moving — it’s exploratory and feeds motor control in real time. Think of holding a fork, typing a text, picking up a ball, or the sole of your foot sensing the ground for balance. Now imagine doing each with numb fingers — that’s the thread of this lesson.

Everyday uses of tactile information

Object manipulation

Holding a fork, gripping a cup, buttoning a shirt — touch tells you contact, slip, and force.

Fine finger skills

Typing, playing an instrument — tactile feedback keeps each contact accurate.

Catching & grasping

Picking up a ball — touch confirms the catch and sets grip.

Balance & locomotion

Foot-sole feedback reports foot–ground contact for standing and walking.

Coming up

Each of these is one input to the Lesson 1 closed loop. To understand how touch delivers it, we need to meet the receptors doing the work — next.

Explore it

The cutaneous mechanoreceptors

Beneath the skin’s surface sit specialized receptors. The skin holds several kinds — temperature receptors, pain receptors (nociceptors), and mechanoreceptors. It’s the cutaneous mechanoreceptors that form the basis of tactile (haptic) perception, and there are four main types, most densely packed in your fingertips.

From skin to cortex

The ascending sensory pathway

Once a receptor fires, the touch signal travels a three-neuron chain to the brain. Each relay — and the distance — is part of why feedback takes time (Lesson 1). Follow the path from the skin up to the somatosensory cortex.

Walking the path, relay by relay

  • 1A skin receptor fires and sends the signal into the 1st-order sensory neuron. Its cell body sits in the dorsal root (sensory) ganglion — just outside the spinal cord — and its axon carries the signal into the spinal cord.
  • 2There it synapses onto a 2nd-order interneuron, which crosses to the other side and ascends the spinothalamic tract, passing up through the medulla oblongata to the thalamus (the brain’s sensory relay station).
  • 3From the thalamus, a 3rd-order interneuron carries the signal the rest of the way to the somatosensory cerebral cortex, where the touch is consciously perceived.
Tie-back to Lesson 1

Three synapses and a long climb from skin to cortex: that distance is one reason tactile feedback is relatively slow (~245 ms to react, from last lesson), and why fast movements can’t wait for it.

Role 2

Movement timing

How might touch help us keep a steady rhythm? Studenka, Zelaznik, and Balasubramaniam (2012) explored this by asking participants to perform rhythmic tapping and circle-drawing tasks, with and without contact with a surface. Let’s see how adding or removing tactile contact changed movement timing.

Event vs. emergent timing

Event timing: the movement is timed to distinct events (the moment of each tap). Emergent timing: timing “falls out” of a continuous movement with no salient events (smooth circle drawing). Adding tactile contact can switch a task from one mode to the other.

Studenka, B. E., Zelaznik, H. N., & Balasubramaniam, R. (2012). The distinction between tapping and circle drawing with and without tactile feedback. The Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology, 65(6), 1086–1100.

How hard should you grip an object? To explore the role of touch, Nowak and Hermsdörfer (2003) studied people with reduced tactile sensitivity in their fingers. Role 4

Postural control & balance

Postural control is the capability to maintain balance when standing. It relies on several senses at once — vision, proprioception, vestibular, and touch — the multisensory integration we flagged in Lesson 1.

Your foot-sole tactile receptors report foot–ground interactions: pressure distribution, shifts, and the edges of your base of support. They help detect and respond to balance changes, contributing substantially to stability while standing and walking.

Lubetzky, A. V., Kelly, J. L., Harel, D., Roginska, A., Hujsak, B. D., Wang, Z., Perlin, K., & Cosetti, M. (2022). Insight into postural control in unilateral sensorineural hearing loss and vestibular hypofunction. PLOS ONE, 17(10), e0276251. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0276251

Clinical relevance — and the lab

Foot-sole sensitivity declines with aging, diabetes mellitus, neuropathies, and as a side effect of chemotherapy — and that decline is linked to poorer balance and higher fall risk. This is exactly the kind of feedback you’ll manipulate in lab.

Viseux, F. J. (2020). The sensory role of the sole of the foot: Review and update on clinical perspectives. Neurophysiologie Clinique, 50(1), 55–68.
