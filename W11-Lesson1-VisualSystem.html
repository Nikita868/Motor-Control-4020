The Visual System

Of all the senses that guide movement, vision is the one we lean on most. This lesson introduces the eye’s anatomy, the rods and cones of the retina, and the pathway that carries visual information from the retina to the visual cortex.

Where we are

Our preferred sense for action

Last unit covered the senses that guide the closed-loop model — touch, proprioception, and the vestibular system. This unit turns to the one the motor system trusts most of all: vision.

The evidence is everywhere in early learning. Beginning piano players stare at their hands. New dancers watch their feet — think back to that first salsa lesson. People recovering from a stroke watch their hands and feet as they move. Early in practice, people often say they simply can’t perform a movement without seeing it.

This lesson

Before we study how vision guides movement (Lessons 2–5), we need the equipment. We’ll walk the light’s path: into the eye, onto the retina, and up to the brain — and see why each part matters for motor control.

The retina: rods and cones

The retina lines the back of the eye. It holds two kinds of photoreceptor — rods and cones — and the difference between them shapes three things that matter for movement. Flip each card:

Flip each card to compare.

Why it matters for motor control

Three consequences follow directly from where the receptors sit: brightness sensitivity (rods let you move in the dark), central vs. peripheral vision (cones for aiming and detail, rods for the edges of the scene), and color (cones) — each useful for different movement tasks.

Two jobs for one retina

Central and peripheral vision

Because cones cluster in the fovea and rods spread to the edges, the retina effectively splits into two systems — a sharp center and a wide, motion-sensitive surround.

Central (foveal) vision

The middle 2–5° of the visual field — a patch about the width of your thumbnail at arm’s length. High cone density means high acuity: the detail you use to aim, read a target, and grasp.

Peripheral vision

Everything outside the center — the visual field spans roughly 200° wide by 160° tall. Lower detail, but excellent for motion and the layout of the scene.

Coming in Lesson 2

What each one actually does for movement — central vision for reaching and staying on a path, peripheral vision for sensing your own moving limbs and the environment, and for detecting self-motion — is picked up in Lesson 3 (posture & optic flow). Here, just hold onto where they come from: cones in the center, rods on the edge.

Try it yourself · Demo 2

How sharp is your peripheral vision?

The last demo showed where you can see. This one shows that seeing something out there isn’t the same as seeing it clearly. You’ll need a partner and a sheet of paper.

  1. Your partner writes one bold letter on a sheet of paper. You don’t look at it yet.
  2. Fix your gaze straight ahead on a spot on the wall — and keep it there the whole time, never glancing at the card.
  3. Your partner brings the card in from the far side of your vision toward the center. Call out when you first see something is there, then again when you can actually read the letter.
What to notice

You spot the card in the periphery long before you can read it — the letter only becomes legible once it’s near straight ahead, landing on the cone-packed fovea. Good detection everywhere, sharp detail only in the center.

Tip

Keep the letter the same size and roughly the same distance each time, so you’re comparing the same thing from trial to trial.

From retina to cortex

The visual pathway

We are going to follow the visual pathway from the retina to the visual cortex. Signals leave each retina through the optic nerve (cranial nerve II). This is the first part of the pathway: the signals then pass through the optic chiasm and optic tracts, relay in the thalamus, and continue to the visual cortex.

Examine the diagram first. Follow one color from the visual field through both eyes to the cortex. Notice where some fibers cross. On the next page, you will trace these routes interactively.

Why I want you to know this

For this lesson, I want you to understand the healthy pathway and trace how visual information reaches the brain. You do not need to identify pathologies or diagnose visual deficits. Learning the normal route now will give you a foundation for future clinical cases affecting vision, such as after a stroke, and help you understand how vision supports everyday movement.

Trace the visual pathway

First, a few definitions.

Visual field means a region of the world you see. Retinal location means the part of the eye receiving its image. Keep your gaze at the center: a target on your left falls on the right half of each retina.

The crossing rule: Nasal retinal fibers cross at the optic chiasm and carry information toward the visual cortex in the opposite hemisphere. Temporal retinal fibers stay on the same side and carry information toward the visual cortex in the same hemisphere.

Here, “same” and “opposite” refer to the eye the fibers come from. These are retinal labels. First locate the image on the retina; then apply the crossing rule.
