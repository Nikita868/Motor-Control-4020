The Speed-Accuracy Trade-off

A familiar idea

Slow down to be accurate

Here’s something everyone already knows: when you want to be more accurate at a skill, it usually makes sense to slow down. Typing a tricky password? You ease off the speed so you don’t make a mistake.

The same bargain shows up everywhere in daily life:

You’ve felt this before

Drive too fast and you might miss your exit. Rush your handwriting and it turns into a scrawl. Hurry a free throw and it clangs off the rim. Speed up, and accuracy tends to suffer — so we trade one for the other.

It feels like plain common sense. But why does it happen? What is the nervous system actually doing when we trade speed for accuracy? In this unit, we’ll dig into the science behind the motor control of the speed–accuracy trade-off.

In the clinic

Most rehab tasks are self-paced — the clinician lets the patient choose a speed so accuracy can succeed: guiding a spoon to the mouth in feeding retraining (OT), stepping onto a floor target during gait (PT), guiding a dental probe to a tooth surface (dentistry). The exception is reactive balance: recovering from a trip or an external push (PT) is skill-determined — the perturbation fixes how fast the step must happen, so speed can’t be traded away. That’s part of why it’s so challenging to retrain.

The key insight

This isn’t carelessness or lack of effort. The trade-off is lawful — so consistent that, as we’ll see, we can write it as an equation and predict how much speed you’ll give up for a given accuracy demand.

A second, independent way to slice it

Spatial vs. temporal accuracy

When we say “accuracy,” we usually picture hitting a spot — that’s spatial accuracy, the first thing that comes to mind. But accuracy can also mean hitting the right moment: temporal accuracy.

Where this is headed

The spatial accuracy trade-off is governed by the laws we study next — Fitts’ Law (Lesson 2), for aiming at a speed you choose, and Schmidt’s Law (Lesson 3), for rapid, ballistic movements. The temporal accuracy trade-off gets its own treatment in Lesson 4.

Why study this at all?

A window into motor control

The speed-accuracy trade-off is more than a curiosity about sports and keyholes. It is one of the most reliable behaviors in all of movement science — and that reliability makes it a window into how the nervous system works. Many of the research studies in this area rely on tasks that require voluntary reaching movements.

The ability to reach and place the hand at a specific location in space is an essential component of daily living — feeding, dressing, and grooming all depend on it. When this ability is compromised, as it commonly is after stroke, everyday independence suffers. That is a central reason for understanding the motor control topics in this unit: they describe the very capacity that rehabilitation aims to restore (McCrea & Eng, 2005).

The model of a fast reaching movement initially proposed by Woodworth (1899) consists of an initial phase in which the movement is planned in advance and launched; then, near the target, the neuromotor system must use feedback to correct its aim — and correcting takes time. Accuracy costs time because making corrections costs time.

The thread of the unit

That “launch, then correct” structure — an open-loop initial phase followed by a closed-loop correcting phase — is the idea the whole unit returns to. It explains Fitts’ Law, Schmidt’s Law, and (in Lesson 4) why the trade-off changes with aging, stroke, and Parkinson’s. Hold onto it.
