Roles of Augmented Feedback

Now that you can name the types, what does augmented feedback actually do for a learner — and when does it start to hurt?

~22 min Augmented feedback · 2 of 4 Schmidt & Lee, Ch. 11

The big picture

Augmented feedback plays four roles

The same feedback can do several jobs at once. Three of them generally help learning — and one is a caution that runs underneath them all:

Helps

1. Informational

Tells the learner what to correct on the next attempt — error correction.

Helps

2. Motivational

Encourages the learner to keep practicing and to engage more fully.

Helps

3. Attentional

Directs where the learner focuses — internally or externally.

Caution

4. Dependency-producing

Given too often, the learner leans on it instead of their own senses.

We’ll take each in turn — and the dependency problem sets up the big idea that closes this lesson: the guidance hypothesis.

Role 1: Informational

Feedback as error correction

The most basic job of KR and KP is to supplement the learner’s own senses with extra information about the movement. That information:

  • •defines the basis for corrections on the next attempt,
  • •brings performance closer to the skilled goal, and
  • •helps the learner problem-solve detecting and correcting their own errors.
Augmented feedback closes the loop: it tells the learner how far the outcome sat from the goal, so the next attempt can correct.
How essential is it?

No feedback, no learning

This isn’t a minor helper. No motor skill learning occurs without feedback of some kind — and when a task offers no reliable intrinsic feedback, augmented feedback becomes the only route to learning.

After practice itself, feedback is the second most important variable for motor learning (Wulf & Shea, 2004).

Think of a task where you genuinely can’t sense the result on your own — a surgeon judging tissue tension through instruments, or a patient with impaired sensation. Without someone or something supplying that information, there’s no error to correct, and no learning.

Wulf, G., & Shea, C. H. (2004). Understanding the role of augmented feedback: The good, the bad and the ugly. In Skill Acquisition in Sport (pp. 145–168). Routledge.  View book →

Just how powerful is it?

People trust feedback — even when it’s wrong

Augmented feedback is so influential that learners will rely on it even when it conflicts with their own senses. A striking demonstration:

Ford et al. (2007) had expert soccer players kick a ball over a barrier, with vision occluded after ball contact. After each kick, they watched a video of the ball flight — but the video was doctored to show the ball 75 cm lower than it actually flew.

Role 2: Motivational (indirect)

Feedback that keeps you coming back

Sometimes feedback helps indirectly — not by carrying error information, but by changing how much the learner practices. KR that shows improvement encourages more practice, and it’s that extra practice that drives the learning.

It can cut both ways

The same feedback can discourage practice for some people — a discouraging number might make a struggling learner give up. Motivation depends on the person.

Role 2: Motivational (direct)

Autonomy makes feedback work better

Feedback can also motivate directly. A robust finding: giving the learner autonomy — some control over when, what, or how they receive feedback — improves learning (Wulf, 2007; Wulf & Lewthwaite, 2016).

Learner autonomy

Letting learners choose when they get feedback boosts motivation — and motivation deepens processing and retention.

Feedback after good trials

Learners given KR on their best attempts learn better than those given KR on their worst (Chiviacowsky & Wulf, 2007).

Role 3: Attentional focusing

KR and KP point attention in different directions

KR → external focus

On the outcome / environment

KR makes the learner think about externally-directed information. Generally good for performance and learning.

KP → internal focus

On the body / movement

KP tends to pull focus inward, onto movement details. Often less ideal — but sometimes necessary, especially for novices.

The practical move

You don’t have to choose. Providing both KP and KR is often the most effective approach — KP to shape the movement, KR to anchor attention on the result.

Role 4: The caution

Feedback can create dependency

Augmented feedback lets a learner correct errors quickly — which is powerful. But it becomes a problem when the learner leans on the augmented source instead of developing their own internal processes to keep the movement on target.

The feedback that helped during practice can quietly become a crutch — and the learner only finds out when it’s taken away.

The big idea

The guidance hypothesis

Augmented feedback can harm learning if it’s provided too frequently, or in a form that’s too easy to use (Salmoni, Schmidt, & Walter, 1984).

The name captures the idea: feedback guides performance — but too much guidance means the learner never has to do the work themselves. Three problems follow:

  • 1The learner becomes dependent — performance collapses when feedback is withdrawn.
  • 2It can interfere with problem-solving — the learner never has to figure things out.
  • 3It can block processing of the learner’s own task-intrinsic feedback.
Where this goes next
