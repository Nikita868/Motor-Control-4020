Turning Passive Lectures into Active Lessons

Dr. Nikita Kuznetsov · Associate Professor-Educator · College of Allied Health Sciences, University of Cincinnati
Fall Golden Apple Teaching Innovations Workshop · 2026

These five interactive lessons make up the content part of the Attention unit of HLSC 4020, an asynchronous online motor control course in the Health Science undergraduate program in RENS at UC. The majority of students enrolled are headed into occupational therapy, medical school, physician assistant programs, and pharmacy. Each one began as in-person lecture slides and classroom activities, and was rebuilt as a self-paced lesson that keeps the “do something, then talk about it” style of the live class.

Try them yourself. They run in any web browser, on a laptop or a phone, with no login.

!
Best way to try them: start with Lesson 1 and do the tapping demo (a laptop trackpad works best). Then open Lesson 2 in the same browser. Its calculator pulls in your own tapping data to compute your dual-task cost. Some screens ask a question before you can continue, just as students experience them.

What makes them active

Demos that generate students' own dataStudents run the experiment on themselves, then analyze their own numbers in the next lesson.
Predict, then revealFlip cards and click-to-reveal tables ask students to commit to an answer before they see it.
Gated knowledge checksApplied, clinical questions must be answered before moving on, so students can't just click through.
Clinical framing throughoutExamples are drawn from rehabilitation and clinical practice so concepts connect to students' future practice.
Linked primary sourcesEvery study cited links to the original paper, so students can go to the source.
Accountability built inEach lesson ends with a completion code students enter in a Canvas quiz.

What students said helped their learning

Exam 1 feedback: 24 nonblank responses in a class of 26 students.

Perceived strengths

  • 11 of 24 mentioned clear, organized, engaging, or interactive lessons.
  • 7 of 24 highlighted applied homework, labs, or study apps.
  • 5 of 24 valued practice quizzes or exam-preparation questions.

Requested improvements

  • 5 of 24 wanted summaries, consolidated notes, or downloadable slides.
  • 3 of 24 requested recorded or narrated explanations.
  • 2 of 24 described scheduling difficulties with partner-dependent labs.
“Putting the concepts into action and reflecting on them, even when they don't quite show up how the theories say they should, has been what reinforces the concepts best for me.”Student response, Exam 1

Design implication: Preserve the interactive format and applied work. Add easier review access and flexible alternatives for partner activities.

About the feedback

The extra-credit question asked what helped learning most and what could be changed or added. Counts reflect descriptive coding of explicit mentions in the 24 supplied responses. Themes overlap. These are student perceptions, not measured learning gains. Instructor-visible, extra-credit feedback may encourage positive responses. Responses concern the course through Exam 1, not the Attention lessons showcased above.

Lesson utilization across the course

35 of 43 lesson pages reached all 26 students. The graph summarizes recorded access to lesson pages by course week.

Broad access, with a localized dip: Week 10 averaged 19 of 26 students per lesson (73%). Week 11 returned to 26 of 26 (100%). Week 12 averaged 20 of 26 (77%).

Access is a participation proxy: Canvas page access does not establish lesson completion, attention, or learning. This export has no date range and later lessons have had less time to accumulate access. Week 13 Attention lessons are not represented in these data.

How they were made

I built these lessons with Claude, an AI assistant from Anthropic, working from my own lecture slides, activities, and research sources. I provided the content, the pedagogical design, and review of every screen, revising each lesson over several rounds of feedback. Each lesson tells students on its title page that it was built with AI tools and reviewed by the instructor.

Technically, each lesson is a single self-contained web page, hosted free on GitHub Pages and embedded in Canvas.
