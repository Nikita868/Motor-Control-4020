Blocked vs. Random Practice

You will learn three 6-key sequences under one practice schedule — either blocked (one sequence at a time) or random (the three interleaved) — as assigned at your station. After a short delay you’ll take a retention test, and your results get added to the class dataset so we can compare the two schedules across everyone.

Your goal

Learn the three sequences by heart. During practice the sequence is shown, but in the retention test at the end it will be hidden and you’ll type each one from memory. Practice as if you’re trying to memorize them — that’s what’s being measured.

How a trial works

A sequence of keys appears. Study it with your eyes only — don’t press the keys while studying. When ready, press Spacebar and type it from memory as fast as you can using the D, F, J, and K keys. The app times you from your first keypress to your last and notes any wrong keys. Try to memorize the sequences as you go — at the end you’ll be tested on them with the keys hidden. What the class data should show

On its own, one run can’t reveal the effect. But once every station’s rows are pooled, the classic contextual interference pattern should appear: the blocked group tends to be faster during practice, yet the random group tends to be faster on the retention test. The schedule that felt harder produces better learning, because interleaving forces you to reconstruct each sequence from memory on nearly every trial. Your lab worksheet asks you to look for this reversal in the pooled dataset.
