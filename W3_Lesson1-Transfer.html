Transfer of Learning

Practice is never the goal. What you actually want is for the skill to show up somewhere else — a new task, a real patient, the world.

~25 min Schmidt & Lee, Ch. 8 Opens this week
Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to…

  • 1Distinguish a retention test from a transfer test, and tell near from far transfer.
  • 2Explain why task similarity drives transfer (the identical elements hypothesis).
  • 3Describe when transfer helps most, and name the factors that promote it.
  • 4Distinguish positive from negative transfer, and read a real transfer study.
Two kinds of test

Retention vs. transfer

You met both briefly in Lesson 3. Here’s the full distinction:

Retention test

Skill assessed under the same conditions as practice.

  • Asks: did the skill persist after practice ended?
  • Retention = the maintenance of a skill over time.
Transfer test

Performance under conditions that differ from practice.

  • Asks: did the learning generalize to something new?
  • Transfer = the influence of prior learning on a new skill, or the same skill in unpracticed conditions.
Everyday transfer

You rely on transfer constantly

Almost nothing you practice is used in exactly the setting you practiced it. Transfer is what makes practice worth doing:

  • →Learning to type on a computer keyboard — then typing on a phone screen.
  • →Learning to drive a manual transmission — then driving an automatic.
  • →Learning a tennis forehand — then playing pickleball.
  • →Rollerblading — then stepping onto ice skates.

How much carries over

Near vs. far transfer

Transfer isn’t all-or-nothing. How much carries over depends on how much the two skills share.

Near Far
Many shared features · more transferFew shared features · less transfer
Near transfer

Many features in common — usually a large amount of transfer.

  • Brushing teeth with the right hand → the left hand
  • A jump shot from one distance → another distance
  • Tennis backhand → pickleball backhand
Far transfer

Few features in common — usually much less transfer.

  • Tennis serve → pickleball serve
  • Tennis two-handed backhand → a baseball swing

Near or far?

For each clinical pair, decide whether moving from the first to the second is near or far transfer. Answer all three to continue.

1. Practicing blood-pressure measurement on a calm classmate → measuring it on a real, anxious patient.

2. Learning a bed-to-wheelchair transfer → performing a wheelchair-to-car-seat transfer.

3. Suturing on a foam practice pad → closing a wound on living human skin.

A judgment call — on a spectrum

All three are near transfer: the core skill is largely the same, with changed context (anxiety, a different surface, tissue that bleeds and tears). But “near” and “far” are ends of a spectrum, not boxes — the anxious patient and living skin add real demands, which is exactly why we don’t treat foam-pad success as proof of clinical readiness. That gap is a transfer problem.

Why near beats far

Similarity drives transfer

You just judged those clinical pairs as “near” because the two skills shared so much. That’s the whole principle:

Transfer between two tasks increases as the similarity between them increases.

Why would sharing features matter? The classic answer is the identical elements hypothesis (Thorndike & Woodworth, 1901):

Identical elements hypothesis

Learning transfers from one skill to another to the extent that the two skills share the same underlying elements — the same movement components, perceptual demands, or concepts. More shared elements → more transfer. It’s not magic generalization; it’s literally reusing parts you already built.

A tennis backhand shares many elements with a pickleball backhand — so it transfers well. Balancing a stick shares almost none with suturing — so it doesn’t.

When transfer helps most

Transfer gives you a head start — not a finish

Transfer is greatest in the early stages of learning a new skill. Prior experience helps you get going — but to actually get good, you have to deliberately practice the target skill itself.

The tennis / pickleball rule of thumb

You can’t become good at tennis by playing pickleball — but pickleball experience will help you get started. The shared elements give you a running start; the differences still have to be trained directly.

Why this matters clinically

Practicing a transfer on a foam pad or a simulator can get a learner started safely — but competence on the real task still requires practice on (or very close to) the real task. Never mistake a good head start for arrival.

What carries across

Three things that promote transfer

When transfer does happen, it’s usually because the two skills share one of these three kinds of element:

1

Fundamental movement patterns

A shared underlying pattern reused across skills. The overarm pattern, for instance, is fundamental to a throw, a tennis serve, and a volleyball spike.

2

Perceptual elements

Shared demands on reading the environment — anticipating and reacting to intercept different kinds of moving balls draws on the same perceptual skill.

3

Strategic & conceptual similarities

Shared rules, concepts, or strategies. Two invasion sports with similar tactics transfer at the level of decisions, even when the movements differ.

Which direction

Positive vs. negative transfer

Prior learning doesn’t only help a new skill. Sometimes it gets in the way.

Positive transfer

Prior learning helps

What you already learned enhances performance of the new skill. Most near transfer is positive — a tennis forehand helps your pickleball forehand.

Negative transfer

Prior learning interferes

Old habits degrade the new skill. A deeply grooved tennis serve can fight the very different motion a pickleball serve needs.

How you actually measure it

Positive or negative compared to what? To a no-practice control group. If a group that trained the first skill does better on the new one than a group that didn’t, that’s positive transfer; worse means negative. The control group is what makes the comparison meaningful — the same logic as the retention design in Lesson 3.

A transfer study

Does VR practice transfer to the real world?

A natural transfer question with a clear design. Michalski and colleagues (2019) asked whether practicing table tennis in virtual reality would improve real table tennis.

1

Baseline

An expert coach rated everyone’s real-world table tennis before training.

2

Training (acquisition)

The VR group played immersive matches against an AI opponent. The control group did not.

3

Transfer test

The same coach re-rated everyone’s real table tennis — performance in a condition never practiced physically.

Michalski, S., Szpak, A., Saredakis, D., Ross, T., Billinghurst, M., & Loetscher, T. (2019). Getting your game on: Using virtual reality to improve real table tennis skills. PLoS ONE, 14(9), e0222351.

The result

The skill transferred

The VR group’s real-world table tennis improved significantly from pre-test to post-test — and clearly more than the no-training control group. Practice in a virtual environment produced positive transfer to physical performance.

How to read the p-values

A p-value estimates how likely a difference this big could have appeared by chance alone. The convention: p < .05 is treated as statistically significant — unlikely to be just chance. Smaller is stronger, so p < .001 is a very confident difference. On this graph:

  • p = .359 at pre-test — the two groups start out the same (well above .05, so no real difference). Good: the groups were comparable before training.
  • p < .001 for each rising line — both groups improved, and neither rise is likely due to chance.
  • p < .001 between groups at post-test — the gap at the end is real. The VR group didn’t just improve; it improved significantly more than the control. That’s the transfer effect.

Why this matters for you

Transfer is the real goal of practice

Every setting where you’ll teach a motor skill is a transfer problem in disguise:

  • →A patient masters a transfer in the clinic — but needs it at home, on different furniture.
  • →A student sutures a foam pad — but must eventually close real tissue.
  • →Rehab exercises are practiced on a mat — but life happens on stairs and sidewalks.
The design implication

If the goal is performance in the real setting, practice should share features with that setting — and success should be judged by a transfer test, not by how good practice looked. This is the through-line of the whole module.
