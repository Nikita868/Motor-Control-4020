Motor Learning Defined

What “learning” actually means — and why the performance you can see is not the same thing.

~25 min Schmidt & Lee, Ch. 8 Prepares the lab
Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to…

  • 1Define motor learning and distinguish it from motor performance.
  • 2Tell a temporary performance variable apart from a relatively permanent learning change.
  • 3Explain why learning cannot be observed directly and must be inferred.
  • 4Identify the general performance characteristics that accompany skill learning.
Before we start

A question to hold in mind

Think of a motor skill you might one day teach someone in your profession — a transfer, an injection, a gait pattern.

Reflect

What would you expect the person to learn from working with you?

And how would you prove — to them, or to a supervisor — that the skill was actually learned, not just performed well once?

Hold your answer. By the end of this lesson you’ll have a precise way to make that case.

The definition

Motor learning is a relatively permanent change in your capability to perform a motor skill, brought about by practice — and it must be inferred from a relatively permanent improvement in performance.

Unpacking it

Three words doing the work

That definition is dense. Three phrases carry all of it:

  • 1Relatively permanent — the change lasts. It survives time and a change of conditions. A one-off good attempt doesn’t count.
  • 2Capability — learning changes what you’re able to do, which is not the same as what you happen to do on any given try.
  • 3Inferred — you can’t see learning directly. You watch performance and reason backward to the capability underneath.
Why this matters clinically

Every claim you’ll ever make that a patient “got better” is really a claim about capability — inferred from performance. The rest of this lesson is about making that inference well.

What’s changing underneath

Learning is a set of processes

When someone learns a motor skill, changes happen at several levels at once — not just “the movement gets better.”

Neural
  • Neuroplasticity — the nervous system physically reorganizes.
Perceptual
  • Better detection of the relevant information in a situation.
  • Better error detection and use of movement feedback.
Cognitive
  • The skill demands less attention to perform.
  • Better decision-making, and better judgment of what counts as accurate.
Neuromotor
  • Muscles generate force more efficiently — less metabolic cost for the same output.
The core distinction

Performance is not learning

Any single performance may not reflect the real skill underneath. It can overshoot or fall short of your true capability, because many temporary factors push it around.

These transient influences are called performance variables:

Performance variables

Caffeine · sleep loss · mood · stress · motivation · extrinsic feedback. Each can lift or depress a single performance without changing what you’ve actually learned.

So a great day at the clinic doesn’t prove learning — and a bad day doesn’t disprove it.

See it in the clinic

When performance improves but nothing is learned

People with Parkinson’s disease can walk dramatically better when there are visual cues on the floor telling them where to step. But the moment the cues are removed, the old gait returns — there was no permanent change in the walking skill.

Cueing lifts performance instantly — and loses it just as fast. Improved performance, no learning.

The other side

Learning leaves a lasting change

When someone genuinely learns, the change is relatively permanent: it survives a shift to new conditions and the passage of time. You can produce the skill even when the temporary factors line up against you —

  • ✓while tired
  • ✓on a real patient rather than a practice dummy
  • ✓under stress, or in a bad mood

That durability — performing despite the performance variables — is the fingerprint of learning.

An analogy

Boiling water vs. boiling an egg

A useful way to feel the difference between temporary and permanent change:

Temporary

Boiling water

Heat it and everything changes — bubbling, steam, motion. Take the heat away and it settles right back to still water. Nothing lasting happened.

Permanent

Boiling an egg

Apply the same heat to an egg and the change doesn’t reverse. Cool it down and it stays cooked. There is no way back.

Performance is boiling water. Learning is the egg.

Knowledge check

Temporary or permanent?

For each, decide whether it’s a temporary performance variable or evidence of a relatively permanent learning change. Answer all three to continue.

1. A student nails a suturing task in lab, having had two cups of coffee and a great night’s sleep.

2. Six weeks after finishing practice, a patient still performs the transfer safely — at home, tired, with no therapist present.

3. A person’s gait looks normal only while floor cues are present, and reverts the instant they’re removed.

The tell

Notice the pattern: the temporary cases lean on a support (caffeine, sleep, floor cues) that, when removed, takes the performance with it. The learning case holds up precisely because the supports are gone. Durability across removed supports is the test.

Why we infer

Learning is never observed directly

The real changes of learning are in the central nervous system — brain plasticity you can’t see. What you can measure is performance over time, and from it you infer that learning occurred.

Analogy

You see someone smile — observable behavior, like performance. From it you infer they’re happy — an internal state, like learning. You never see the happiness itself; you reason to it from what you can see.

This is why the whole field leans on careful study designs: if learning can only be inferred, the inference has to be built well. That’s the next lesson.

What learning looks like over time

Five signs of skill learning

As a skill is genuinely learned, performance tends to show five characteristics:

1

Improvement

Performance gets better over time.

2

Consistency

It becomes more reliable, trial to trial.

3

Persistence

Gains hold up over longer and longer stretches.

4

Adaptability

The skill works across varied contexts.

5

Less attention

It takes less mental effort to perform.
