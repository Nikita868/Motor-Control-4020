Measuring Attention Demands

If attention is a limited resource, how do we measure how much a task demands? This lesson covers the two workhorse methods — the dual-task paradigm and probe reaction time — and why they matter at the bedside.

Method 1

The dual-task paradigm

The core idea is simple: give someone a second task and see what it costs. If a task demands a lot of attention, loading it up should make performance drop.

Primary task

The task we care about — e.g., walking.

Secondary task

Added to load attention — e.g., counting backward, answering questions.

We compare performance under single-task and dual-task conditions. Two things shape what we find:

  • 1Priority instructions matter. What you tell people to focus on changes which task suffers.
  • 2Measure both tasks. Interference often goes both ways — “mutual interference.” If you only watch one task, you miss where the cost landed.
Putting a number on the cost

Dual-task cost (DTC)

Dual-task cost expresses, as a percentage, how much performance changed when the second task was added. The form depends on whether higher or lower is the good direction for that measure.

DTC (%) = (Single − Dual) / Single × 100
use when higher is better — speed, correct responses, taps
DTC (%) = (Dual − Single) / Single × 100
flip it when lower is better — time, errors
Read it the same way either way

A positive DTC means worse performance under dual-task conditions. (A negative DTC would mean the person actually did better with the second task — rare, but possible.)

Bottom line — the honest version

Dual-task cost is a promising marker of fall risk — but testing methods aren’t standardized enough yet for firm clinical cut-offs. Use it as one signal among many, not a verdict.

Lundin-Olsson, L., et al. (1997). Stops walking when talking as a predictor of falls in elderly people. Lancet, 349, 617. doi:10.1016/S0140-6736(97)24009-2 · Beauchet, O., et al. (2009). Stops walking when talking: a predictor of falls in older adults? Eur. J. Neurology, 16, 786–795. doi:10.1111/j.1468-1331.2009.02612.x · Muir-Hunter, S. W., & Wittwer, J. E. (2016). Dual-task testing to predict falls in community-dwelling older adults. Physiotherapy, 102, 29–40. doi:10.1016/j.physio.2015.04.011

Method 2

Probe reaction time

The dual-task paradigm asks whether a task is demanding. Probe reaction time asks when — it maps how attention demand rises and falls within a single movement.

While doing a primary task, the person responds to a probe (e.g., a tone) as fast as possible. Probes are fired at different moments, and the logic is direct:

The rule

Longer probe RT = more attention demanded by the primary task at that instant.

Probe RT · a rehab example

Balance takes attention (Lajoie et al., 1993)

Lajoie and colleagues used probe RT to show, concretely, that staying upright is not “free” — it draws on attention, and more so the harder the balance demand. Predict each result, then flip both boxes.

Why this matters clinically

If balance itself consumes attention, then a patient with reduced balance (after stroke, with neuropathy, in aging) has less attention left over for anything else — which is exactly why dual-task walking exposes their risk.

Lajoie, Y., Teasdale, N., Bard, C., & Fleury, M. (1993). Attentional demands for static and dynamic equilibrium. Experimental Brain Research, 97(1), 139–144. doi:10.1007/BF00228824
