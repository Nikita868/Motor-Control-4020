Where we left off

Turning the question around

In Lesson 2, Fitts fixed the accuracy demand (a target of width W at distance A) and measured the cost in time. The harder the target, the longer the movement took.

But think about a boxer’s jab, a drummer’s stroke, a dart throw, hammering a nail, a quick dialing gesture. Here the performer chooses the speed freely and just lets the movement fly. Now time isn’t the cost — error is.

In this lesson we’ll pin down exactly how that error depends on speed — and you’ll generate the relationship yourself with a hands-on demo.

The paradigm

One fast movement, measured where it lands

To isolate how speed affects accuracy, Schmidt, Zelaznik, Hawkins, Frank, and Quinn (1979) studied a task that strips the problem down to a single aiming movement. Rather than having people tap back and forth (as Fitts did), they asked participants to make one rapid reach from a start position to a target — and crucially, they controlled how fast the movement had to be, then watched what happened to accuracy.

1

The task

Move to the target so that you reach it within a specified time. The instruction is a movement time, not a target size — and different conditions ask for different times (and distances).

2

Very fast movements

The required times are short, so the reach is ballistic — launched and completed in a fraction of a second.

3

No ongoing error correction

At these speeds, correcting the movement mid-flight is typically not possible. Whatever accuracy you get is baked into the launch itself — the task is essentially open-loop.

What gets measured is the spread of endpoints around the target — the spatial variability, written as We (effective target width).

Rapid single aiming movements are too brief for corrective visual feedback, which is what makes them a clean window on the ballistic impulse (Zelaznik et al., 1981; Schmidt et al., 1979).

The law

Faster movements scatter more

Schmidt et al. (1979) found a strikingly simple pattern across these single-aiming movements: the spread of endpoints grows linearly with the speed of the movement.

We = c + k · (A / MT)
effective target width = c (intercept) + k (slope) × (amplitude ÷ movement time)
In plain words

Here’s the key link: A ÷ MT is just average speed — distance over time. So the equation says endpoint scatter rises in a straight line with how fast you move. Move the same distance in less time (smaller MT), or cover more distance in the same time (larger A), and velocity goes up — and so does the error. (We write the constants as c and k to keep them distinct from Fitts’ a and b.)

What to notice

1. Compare the three speeds. The 300, 250, and 200 ms conditions all use the same distance, so a shorter movement time means a greater average speed.

2. Watch the oval’s horizontal width. Does the oval get wider (left–right) as you move faster? If so, that fits Schmidt’s Law: greater speed goes with greater endpoint variability.

3. Read the oval’s shape. A larger oval = more overall scatter. Its horizontal width is variability along the direction of movement; its vertical height is scatter along the target line. An oval sitting to the left or right of the target line means a tendency to undershoot or overshoot — that’s a bias, which is different from variability.

4. Expect some noise. Your result may not be clean — there are only five counted trials per condition, and your input device and the time limit affect the outcome. Check the actual mean movement times, not just the condition labels.

About the ovals: These are approximate 95% data ellipses based on the sample covariance and a bivariate-normal model. They describe endpoint spread, not a 95% confidence interval for the mean, and do not guarantee that exactly 95% of these five taps fall inside. With so few trials, their size and shape are uncertain. A line or point indicates little or no spread in one or both directions.

The explanation · impulse variability

Why do rapid movements become variable?

Schmidt et al. (1979) designed the rapid-aiming experiments to test the hypothesis that variability in the initial force impulse contributes to the speed–accuracy trade-off. Brief, ballistic movements helped isolate the initial impulse by limiting the opportunity for corrective feedback during the movement.

A motor program organizes the order and timing of muscle activity and the forces needed for a movement. But the forces actually produced vary slightly from one attempt to the next, even when we intend to repeat exactly the same action.

Feedback can help correct some errors when there is enough time. The impulse-variability explanation focuses on how variability is generated in the movement itself, rather than treating all inaccuracy as a failure to use feedback.

Beyond reaching · force variability

Does more force always mean more variability?

The classic view

Classical motor-output variability models emphasized an approximately linear relationship between force output and absolute force variability. Signal-dependent noise became an influential explanation for why larger motor commands produce more variable outcomes.

Where the straight line breaks down

Studies of isometric and dynamic contractions show the linear relationship can fail at high force. Variability may level off and even decrease near maximum force (Sherwood & Schmidt, 1980), and it also depends on how the force is produced — increasing vs. decreasing force can differ even at low levels (Masumoto & Inui, 2010). In sustained isometric tasks a sigmoidal pattern appears: a steeper rise near 65% of maximum, then a reduced rate of increase above ~80% (Novak et al., 2021).

A question to carry forward

If variability levels off or even decreases near maximum effort, should we always expect a more forceful movement to be less accurate? And remember: force variability alone doesn’t settle spatial accuracy — we have to look at the outcome actually measured. That sets up the next screen on very forceful movements.

Exceptions to the usual pattern

When faster does not necessarily mean less accurate

Fitts’ and Schmidt’s laws describe important speed–accuracy relationships, but these patterns do not apply in the same way to every task. One clear exception involves extremely rapid, forceful actions performed at close to maximum effort.

Very forceful movements

Kicking a football or throwing a fastball requires strong muscle contractions and movement speeds close to maximum. These actions still require precision. However, changing speed near maximum effort can affect performance differently than changing speed in a less forceful aiming task.

Very forceful movements performed at nearly maximal speed can be an exception to the usual speed–accuracy trade-off.

Lesson 3 · wrap-up

What to carry forward

1

Schmidt’s Law

In rapid, open-loop aiming, endpoint scatter grows linearly with movement speed: We = c + k·(A/MT), and A/MT is just velocity.

2

The mechanism

Larger force requirements generally bring greater output variability over the submaximal range. Variations in force magnitude and duration contribute to impulse variability.

3

Fitts vs. Schmidt

Same trade-off, opposite bookkeeping: Fitts prices accuracy in time, Schmidt prices speed in spatial error.

4

You can see it in data

Rapid aiming often shows a roughly linear relation between speed and endpoint spread. Near maximum force, however, force variability may plateau or decrease. The task and the outcome measured matter.
