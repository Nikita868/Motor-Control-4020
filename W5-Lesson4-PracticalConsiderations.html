Consideration 2

Should you give KP or KR?

Recall from Lesson 1: KR is about the result; KP is about the movement. Which should you use? It depends on the skill and the learner’s expertise — there’s no single winner.

A useful starting fact

KP gives more precise information about how to change a movement than KR does — and in applied coaching and clinical settings, KP is actually the more common of the two (Fishman & Tobey, 1978).

Let’s work through when each one shines — starting with KP.

Oppici, L., Dix, A., & Narciss, S. (2024). When is knowledge of performance (KP) superior to knowledge of results (KR) in promoting motor skill learning? A systematic review. International Review of Sport and Exercise Psychology, 17(1), 182–207.  View study →

When is KP beneficial?  ·  Case 1 of 2

1Skills with a specific movement requirement

Some skills are defined by how the body moves — gymnastics, ballet, diving. The goal isn’t just an outcome; it’s a particular movement form. Here KP is essential: KR (“you landed the cartwheel”) can’t tell the learner their legs weren’t straight.

Two flavors of KP

Descriptive vs. prescriptive KP

Descriptive KP

Names the error

Describes what went wrong. “Your knees weren’t bent.” Better for more skilled learners — they can figure out the fix themselves.

Prescriptive KP

Names the error and the fix

Describes what went wrong and how to correct it. “Bend your knees more!” Better for beginners — they may not know the fix yet.

The case for KR

What KR does well

KR has a hidden strength: because it reports only the outcome — not what to do — it forces the learner to engage problem-solving. They have to discover the fix themselves.

Who it suits

That discovery process works best for learners with more experience on a skill. It’s not ideal for novices on complex skills — they may not have the foundation to solve the problem on their own, and can flounder.

KR also fits simple, single-criterion tasks

When a skill has one clear goal criterion — hitting a dartboard, a beanbag toss to a target, or a positioning task like moving a lever to a precise location — the outcome is the whole point. KR (“you were 3 cm short”) tells the learner exactly what they need, and there’s little movement form to describe.

Historically, in fact, most motor learning research focused on KR rather than KP — only more recently has KP’s role been reviewed systematically (Oppici et al., 2024).

Oppici, L., Dix, A., & Narciss, S. (2024). When is knowledge of performance (KP) superior to knowledge of results (KR) in promoting motor skill learning? A systematic review. International Review of Sport and Exercise Psychology, 17(1), 182–207.  View study →

The resolution

Often, use both

KP vs. KR isn’t really a competition. The research points to a practical answer:

Combined prescriptive KP + KR is more effective than providing either one alone (Oppici et al., 2021).

Bottom line

Both KP and KR can be valuable for motor learning. The skill is matching them to the task and the learner’s stage — and often, giving the movement fix (KP) alongside the outcome (KR).

Consideration 3

How precise should feedback be?

Feedback can be delivered at different levels of precision:

Quantitative

Exact numbers

“You overshot by 3 cm.” · “You walked 3 s faster.” · “Bend your knee 5° more.”

Qualitative

General direction

“You overshot a bit.” · “That was much faster.” · “Bend your knee a bit more.”

Which is better? As with everything in this module — it depends on the learner.

Matching precision to skill

Early = qualitative, skilled = quantitative

Early in practice

Qualitative is better

Errors are large, so exact precision wouldn’t be used effectively anyway — and beginners attend to general, qualitative information.

Skilled performers

Quantitative can help

Precise feedback becomes useful because skilled performers have the fine movement control to actually act on exact numbers.

Module wrap-up

Key takeaways: effective augmented feedback

Across all four lessons, augmented feedback best supports learning when it…

  • 1Provides informational value without creating over-dependence.
  • 2Encourages autonomy and motivation.
  • 3Matches the learner’s stage and the skill’s complexity.
  • 4Balances KR and KP — and often combines them.
That completes the augmented feedback module

You can now classify feedback, explain what it does, decide how much to give, and choose its content, precision, and form for a specific learner. That’s the full toolkit for using feedback as a clinician or coach.
