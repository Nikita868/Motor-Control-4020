Part 1 · What is a theory?

What makes a theory a good one?

A theory helps us understand a phenomenon — it explains why things behave as they do. To be scientifically useful, it must do three things (Hawking, 1996; Popper, 1959). Flip each card for an example.

The one that trips people up

Falsifiability (Popper) is the key. If no possible observation could ever contradict a claim, it isn’t a scientific theory — it’s unfalsifiable. Good theories stick their necks out and risk being wrong.

Prediction in action

A theory that stuck its neck out

When Mendeleev organized the elements by atomic mass in 1869, his periodic table did something remarkable: it left gaps and predicted the properties of elements no one had discovered yet.

A little background

Dmitri Ivanovich Mendeleev, a Russian chemist born in 1834. He built upon earlier work by chemists like John Dalton and Antoine Lavoisier, but his table was unique in that it organized elements according to increasing atomic mass.

Those predicted elements — gallium, germanium — were later found, with almost exactly the properties the table forecast.

That’s a theory earning its keep: it didn’t just describe the known elements, it made a risky, testable prediction about unknown ones — and passed. Hold that standard in mind as we judge the motor control theories ahead.

Why we need theory

The questions a theory of motor control must answer

That simple nose-touch opens onto a whole set of puzzles. A good theory of motor control and learning should help explain all of these:

  • •How does the nervous system coordinate so many moving parts?
  • •Why is there so much variability in how we move?
  • •Why does transfer work — using a skill in a new situation?
  • •Why do practice and feedback conditions change learning the way they do?
  • •How do neurological disorders disrupt movement — and how do we restore it?

You’ve met several of these already this semester. Theory is what ties them together instead of leaving them as separate facts.

The clinical payoff

Why should a clinician care about theory?

Because theory turns trial and error into a systematic approach. Take a stroke survivor relearning to reach for a cup — theory guides every step. Flip each card to see it applied to the case.

Part 2 · The core puzzle

The puzzles every theory must solve

Across the whole field, three puzzles keep coming back. Every theory you’ll meet is, at heart, an attempt to solve them:

Degrees of freedom

Too many parts

How does the nervous system organize so many independently-moving components?

Variability

Never the same twice

Why is no movement ever repeated exactly — yet we still hit the goal?

Novelty & transfer: how do we perform movements we’ve never practiced before?

The first of these — degrees of freedom — is the big one. It’s the thread we’ll follow through all four lessons.

Degrees of freedom

Degrees of freedom (DOF) = the number of independent ways the body’s components can move to perform a task. Let’s count them at the joints of a single arm. Tap the DOF for each joint (how many independent axes it rotates about):

The central problem

Bernstein’s degrees-of-freedom problem

Seven DOF in one arm — and that’s just the major joints. The body has far more degrees of freedom than any task strictly requires. That surplus is the whole problem:

Bernstein’s problem: with so many more DOF than needed, how does the nervous system coordinate and control them all to produce smooth, goal-directed movement?

Nikolai Bernstein (1896–1966)

Russian neurophysiologist who framed the central question of motor control. Nearly every theory in this module is an attempt to answer his problem.

The solution concept

Coordination = tying DOF together

Coordination is the organization of movement components into a controllable unit (Bernstein, 1967; Turvey, 1990). Note this isn’t the everyday meaning of “coordination” — it’s about reducing the number of things you have to control. Think about steering a car: if you had to control a car whose four wheels each steered independently, it would be a 4-DOF steering nightmare. Instead, we lock the rear wheels and couple the front pair — collapsing 4 DOF into 1 (left/right). That coupling is coordination, and it makes the car far easier to control:

Where this module goes

Two families of answers

How does the nervous system tame all those degrees of freedom? The field splits into two big camps — and the next three lessons take you through them:

Motor program theories

“Intelligent executive”

Coordination comes from stored instructions in the central nervous system. The brain is in charge. (Lesson 2)

Dynamical systems theories

“Intelligent body”

Coordination self-organizes from the physics of the body interacting with its environment. (Lesson 3)

Then, in Lesson 4, we’ll look at two stage theories describing how learning unfolds over time — and a synthesis that puts it all to work.
