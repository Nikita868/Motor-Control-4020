Module 1 · Lesson 2

Classifying Motor Skills

Four ways to read the demands of a task — building up to Gentile’s taxonomy, the tool you’ll use in Lab 1.

~30 min Schmidt & Lee, Ch. 1 Prepares Lab 1
Start here

Why classify a skill at all?

Before you can teach or rehabilitate a movement, you need to know what it demands of the learner.

Classification tells you the cognitive load (attention, memory, decision-making) and the physical control a task requires — systematically, rather than by gut feel.

Why it matters clinically

The same classification that describes a skill also tells you how to progress practice and how to chart a patient’s recovery. Classification isn’t labeling for its own sake — it’s the first step of program design.

You’ll meet four motor skill classification systems. The first three are one-dimensional — each sorts skills along a single relevant dimension. The fourth — Gentile’s taxonomy — is two-dimensional, combining dimensions into a full map.

System 1 · Musculature

Gross vs. fine motor skills

Clinicians — PTs and OTs especially — frequently classify motor skills by the size of the musculature doing the work.

GROSSFINE
Large musculatureSmall musculature
Many real skills sit in the middle — pitching or rock climbing need both
Gross

Large muscles

Walking, jumping, squatting, swimming

System 2 · Structure in time

Discrete, serial, and continuous

This classification system sorts skills by where the action begins and ends. It’s used mostly in motor learning and control research, because the neural control of discrete, continuous, and serial actions shows different patterns of brain activation. Select each to reveal how it’s defined.

Watch the boundary

Serial skills are the tricky middle case — discrete movements chained, so they’re often mislabeled continuous. Two tests: can you point to distinct sub-actions with their own endpoints? And is there usually a slight pause between the movements? If yes, it’s serial.

Knowledge check

Discrete, serial, or continuous?

Answer both to continue.

1. Shifting gears in a manual car — clutch in, move the stick, clutch out.

2. Typing an unfamiliar word on a keyboard — you hit each letter deliberately, with a slight pause between keystrokes as you work out what comes next.

The pattern

Both are serial: separate discrete actions, each with its own endpoint, strung together in a required order — with those small pauses between them. Notice that the same skill can shift category with expertise: a practiced typist producing a familiar word runs the keystrokes together so fluently that the pauses vanish, and it starts to look continuous.

Answer both to continue.

System 3 · The environment

First, what the environment demands

The third classification system sorts skills by the environmental context in which they’re performed. Before we can use it, we need one term.

The features of the environment that a movement must conform to are called the regulatory conditions — they regulate the action, in the sense that your movement has to be shaped to fit them.

  • The supporting surface — the floor, ground, or surface you stand, sit, or move on, and whether it is level, uneven, slippery, or itself moving.
  • The objects involved — anything you handle or interact with: a cup, a ball, a walker, a door.
  • Other people or animals — teammates, opponents, pedestrians, a patient, a dog.
Try it on one skill

Pouring a glass of water at your kitchen counter. The surface is a level floor. The objects are a jug and a glass. There are no other people or animals involved. Now hold those three in mind — the next screen asks the only question that matters about them.

System 3 · The environment

Open vs. closed skills

Here is the question: are the regulatory conditions stationary, or are they in motion?

CLOSEDOPEN
Stationary environmentEnvironment in motion
The performer starts the action  |  the environment dictates timing
Why this one matters most

Open skills are typically more demanding: tracking moving objects costs attention, and the timing is dictated to you rather than chosen. But that doesn’t mean every learner should start closed and work outward — a skill that is performed in an open environment often has to be practiced in one, or the practice won’t transfer. Deciding when to simplify the environment and when to preserve its demands is a judgment you’ll return to all semester.

Knowledge check

Open or closed?

Ask each time: is something in the regulatory conditions moving? Answer all four to continue.

1. A golfer hits a shot during a round on a calm course.

2. Performing a bench press in the gym.

3. Making a pass to a teammate during soccer practice.

4. Running cross-country over trail and uneven ground — compared with running on a track.

The pattern

Notice that the same action can fall on either side depending on where it’s performed — running is the clearest case. Open vs. closed isn’t a property of the movement; it’s a property of the environment the movement happens in. That’s also why these sit on a continuum rather than in two boxes.

Answer all four to continue.

Now combine them

Gentile’s taxonomy doesn’t replace these systems — it crosses two of them into a single map of skill difficulty.

We’ll build it one dimension at a time.

Gentile’s taxonomy · Dimension 1 of 2

Environmental context

Antoinette Gentile (2000) classified every motor skill along two dimensions. The first extends the open/closed idea you just learned — split into two sub-questions.

1 Environmental context
Regulatory conditionsStationary or in-motion? (This is closed vs. open.)
Inter-trial variabilityDo conditions stay the same from one attempt to the next, or change each time?
Inter-trial variability, concretely

Walking the same clear hallway every day has no inter-trial variability — each trip demands essentially the same movements. Walking through a room where the furniture is rearranged each time has variability — every trip requires a different path and different steps. The regulatory conditions can be closed in both cases, yet the demand on the walker is very different.

Gentile’s taxonomy · Dimension 2 of 2

Function of the action

The second dimension describes the performer — again as two sub-questions.

2 Function of the action
Body orientationIs the body stationary (static) or does it change location (transport) during the skill?
Object manipulationIs the performer handling an object, or none at all?
The organizing principle

Cross the two dimensions and you get sixteen categories. Read them top-left to bottom-right: simplest (stable world, still body, no object, no variability) climbing to most complex (moving world, moving body, object to manage, variability every trial).

Walk the progression

The full taxonomy

Both dimensions are now in place. Columns are the function of the action; rows are the environmental context. Every cell is the same skill — hitting a baseball — modified to fit that category.

Work through the cells in order: left to right, then down a row — 1A → 1B → 1C → 1D → 2A and so on. That path is the taxonomy’s order of difficulty, from least demanding to most.

You just walked the difficulty gradient

From a bare swing with nothing to react to, all the way to hitting a live pitcher’s throw while stepping forward. Nothing about the batter changed — only what the environment demanded and what the body had to manage. That ordering is the taxonomy’s practical payoff: it tells you where a learner currently succeeds and which single change to make next.

Each cell is a distinct combination of the two dimensions.

Apply it

One skill, all four systems

A player shoots a basketball free throw: she steps to the line, sets herself, and shoots — alone, unguarded, on a still court, with the same routine every time.

This is exactly what Lab 1 will ask of you. Classify the free throw on each system below. Answer all four to continue.

1. Musculature — gross, fine, or both?

2. Structure in time — discrete, serial, or continuous?

3. Environment — open or closed?

4. Gentile’s taxonomy — the court is stable and her routine is identical every time (no inter-trial variability), her body stays in place, and she handles a ball. Which cell?

You just did Lab 1’s core move

Gross and fine, discrete, closed, cell 1B — low-difficulty on every dimension. But notice that a free throw isn’t a stepping stone to the rest of basketball; it’s its own skill, practiced as it’s performed. Now picture a different shot — contested, off the dribble, moving toward the basket: it slides toward open, serial, and the bottom-right of Gentile’s grid, and it has to be practiced under those conditions to transfer. Classifying the skill tells you what the practice has to contain.
