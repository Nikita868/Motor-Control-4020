Experimental Evidence for Motor Programs

We’ve defined the motor program and seen where it fits. But is it real? This lesson walks through the classic studies — and one gruesome legend — that convinced the field.

~20 min Motor programs · 3 of 4 Wadman; Taub; Slater-Hammel
What this lesson does

From definition to evidence

A good theory makes testable predictions. If a movement really is driven by a pre-structured program that runs without feedback, then certain things should be true — and clever experiments can check them.

The claim on trial

What would we expect to see?

Keele’s definition says a motor program is structured before the movement and runs uninfluenced by peripheral feedback. If that’s true, two things should follow. Flip each card to see the prediction.

How to read the rest of this lesson

Each study below is a different way of testing one of these predictions. As you go, ask: which prediction is this one checking?

A legend to set the scene

The pirate who walked without a head

Klaus Störtebeker was a German pirate executed in Hamburg in 1401. According to legend, before his beheading he struck a bargain with the mayor: any of his crew he could walk past after losing his head would be set free.

As the story goes, his headless body rose and walked past eleven of his men before the executioner tripped him with an outstretched foot.

Why tell this story?

It’s almost certainly a tall tale — not evidence of anything. But it’s a vivid picture of the very idea we’re testing: a highly practiced action (walking) that, once released, seems to run to completion on its own, with no brain “watching” and steering it. Keep that image in mind; the real experiments below ask whether anything like it actually holds up.

Source: “Klaus Störtebeker,” Wikipedia — recounted here as folklore, not fact.

A closer look · study 1 (Wadman)

The task: a fast arm movement, sometimes blocked

Participants made a rapid elbow-extension movement toward a target while EMG recorded the arm muscles. On some trials, the limb was mechanically blocked right at the start — it couldn’t actually move.

The logic

If the muscle commands are pre-planned, then blocking the limb shouldn’t change them — at least not right away. Feedback saying “the arm isn’t moving” would take time to arrive and act. So: does the blocked-trial EMG match the normal-trial EMG at first?

A closer look · study 1 (Wadman)

First, the normal movement (in red)

Look at the red (Normal) traces. A fast movement has a stereotyped three-burst pattern:

  • 1Initial agonist burst (triceps) — fires first to accelerate the limb.
  • 2Antagonist burst (biceps) — fires next to decelerate and stop it at the target.
  • 3Stabilizing burst (agonist again) — settles the limb on target.
Notice the braking

The antagonist brake fires before the limb could possibly have reported “I’m approaching the target.” The stop was planned from the beginning.

A closer look · study 1 (Wadman)

Now compare the blocked movement (in blue)

Here is the key result. Overlay the blue (Blocked) trace on the red. For roughly the first 100 ms, the two are essentially identical — even though in the blue condition the arm never moved at all.

For the first ~100 ms the blocked arm is already stopped — yet the muscles fire exactly as if it were moving normally. Those commands can’t be coming from feedback; they were programmed in advance.

Only after ~100–120 ms does the blocked EMG start to differ — that’s when sensory feedback (“the arm isn’t where it should be”) finally loops back and alters the pattern. The first chunk of the movement is open-loop, under the motor program; feedback only takes over later.

A closer look · study 2 (Taub)

Deafferentation: movement without sensation

Deafferentation means surgically cutting the dorsal (sensory) roots where they enter the spinal cord. The limb can still be moved by motor nerves, but the brain no longer receives any sensory feedback from it — no touch, no proprioception.

Taub found that monkeys could still perform learned movements of a deafferented limb. Sensory feedback from the moving limb is not absolutely required to produce the movement — the plan can drive the muscles on its own.

A note on ethics

Taub’s deafferentation work is frequently cited as a classic ethical dilemma in science — a case where severe animal suffering directly paved the way for a major medical breakthrough (constraint-induced movement therapy) that now benefits hundreds of thousands of paralyzed people. The “Silver Spring monkeys” case that grew out of this research helped launch the modern animal-rights movement. Worth pausing on as you read the result. Read more (The New Yorker)

Citation

Taub, E. (1976). Movement in nonhuman primates deprived of somatosensory feedback. Exercise and Sport Sciences Reviews, 4(1), 335–374.

A closer look · study 3 (Slater-Hammel)

Once started, it’s hard to stop

Another classic study on motor programs is by Slater-Hammel (1960). In this study, a clock hand swept toward the “800 ms” mark (panel a). Participants planned to lift a finger exactly at 800 — but on some trials the hand stopped early, and they had to cancel the lift.

What it means

It takes roughly 200 ms to cancel an already-prepared response. Once the program is launched, it tends to play out in full — the measurable version of the Störtebeker image.

A closer look · study 4 (StartReact)

A startle releases the program early

In the StartReact effect, pairing the “go” signal with a sudden loud (startling) sound makes a prepared movement come out sooner — without changing the movement itself (Valls-Solé et al., 1995).

The interpretation

If a startle can release the movement early but intact, the whole plan must have been prepared and waiting — the startle just pulls the trigger sooner. You can’t speed up something that hasn’t been assembled yet.

An example from Capstone research in our department

A Capstone research project in our department (HLSC 5044 — where students run independent-study projects with faculty) tested this with a vertical jump. Participants jumped as high as possible after a beep, with EMG on the jumping muscles and jump height measured by a TENDO unit. On some trials, the beep was unexpectedly loud.

On loud-beep trials, people initiated the jump sooner — but jump height was unchanged. The motor program was released earlier, not modified. The whole plan was packaged and ready to fire.

Citation & further reading

Valls-Solé, J., Solé, A., Valldeoriola, F., Muñoz, E., Gonzalez, L. E., & Tolosa, E. S. (1995). Reaction time and acoustic startle in normal human subjects. Neuroscience Letters, 195(2), 97–100. https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-3940(94)11790-P

A closer look · study 5 (CPGs)

Central pattern generators: rhythm from the spinal cord

A Central Pattern Generator (CPG) is a small neural circuit — in the spinal cord or brainstem — that produces rhythmic, patterned output on its own, with no sensory feedback and no conscious control.

CPG vs. motor program

The two ideas are close cousins. A motor program is usually for learned actions (kicking, throwing); a CPG is for more genetically built-in, rhythmic ones (locomotion, chewing, breathing). Both show movement organized centrally, without needing feedback. Brown (1911).

The point

Brown (1911) showed that a deafferented, decerebrate cat still produced alternating stepping. This demo captures why that result mattered: a small central circuit can manufacture a patterned, rhythmic output with no rhythmic input driving it — the clearest case of movement organized from the center outward.

Stepping back

What the evidence does — and doesn’t — show

Taken together, these studies make a strong case that pre-structured, centrally organized commands drive fast movements, at least for the first fraction of a second, without needing feedback.

What it supports

Programs are real

Movements can be organized in advance and run open-loop — across humans, monkeys, and spinal circuits.

But strong evidence that programs exist leaves two big questions unanswered:

Open question 1

How are they stored?

It’s implausible to store a separate program for every variation of every movement — the storage problem.

Open question 2

How do new ones develop?

How do we produce a movement we’ve never made before, if there’s no program for it yet? The novelty problem.

Where this goes

Both of these are taken up in Lesson 4, where Schmidt’s generalized motor program and schema theory offer a way out.
