Retention of Skill

Why some skills survive long periods without practice, why others fade, and why a rough first attempt does not always mean the skill was forgotten.

~15–20 min Builds on retention-test design Closes Module 2

A familiar puzzle

Why do some things seem impossible to forget?

You may go years without riding a bicycle and still be able to get back on. Yet a hotel-room number can disappear from memory within days.

Retention is not the same for every learned task. How much survives a break depends partly on how long the break is and what kind of task was learned.

A key term

Retention interval

The retention interval is the time between the end of practice and the later retention test.

Why it matters

A short interval may leave performance almost unchanged. A long interval gives more opportunity for retention loss — but the amount of loss depends strongly on the task.

Study 1 · A discrete task

Switch–light combinations were forgotten fairly quickly

This classic early study by Neumann and Ammons (1957) provides a clear demonstration of how retention can change as the time away from a task increases. The researchers used a relatively simple discrete task so they could compare performance after several very different retention intervals.

Participants learned which of eight switches corresponded to eight lights. The task had a substantial verbal-memory component: the learner had to remember specific pairings. Practice continued until participants completed two errorless trials.

At that point, different groups stopped practicing and returned for a retention test after 1 minute, 20 minutes, 2 days, 7 weeks, or 1 year. This design makes it possible to see what happens to the same learned task as the retention interval becomes progressively longer.

Notice one more thing

After a year, participants were nearly back to their starting level on the first retention trial — but they improved faster than during original practice. That suggests that not everything had been lost.

Study 2 · A continuous tracking task

Complex tracking was retained remarkably well

Another classic study, by Fleishman and Parker (1962), provides an important contrast. Rather than testing a discrete task with a strong memory component, they examined retention of a much more continuous, coordinated motor skill.

Participants performed a complex tracking task using the hands and feet with aircraft-style stick and rudder controls. After extensive training, retention was tested following very long periods without practice: 9 months, 12 months, or 24 months.

Retention was tested after 9 months, 12 months, or 24 months without practice.

The contrast

The long retention interval alone did not guarantee large forgetting. The type of skill mattered.

What the two studies suggest

Task characteristics shape retention

Discrete / memory-heavy task

The switch–light task depended on remembering specific pairings and showed substantial loss as the retention interval grew.

Pattern: performance was more vulnerable to forgetting.

Continuous / rhythmic tracking task

The coordinated tracking task involved an ongoing pattern of movement and was retained well across very long intervals.

Pattern: the motor skill proved remarkably durable.

These studies illustrate a broad contrast rather than a rule that every discrete skill is forgotten and every continuous skill is retained. The central point is that retention depends on the characteristics of the task.

A different kind of retention deficit

Warm-up decrement

Sometimes performance is poor only at the very beginning after a break. A musician struggles to find the rhythm, a goalie enters late and is not ready for the speed of play, or a basketball player feels off on the first free throw.

Warm-up decrement is a temporary depression in performance at the start of activity after time away from the task.

The key clue is that the deficit disappears quickly after a few practice attempts.

Not physiological warm-up

Here, “warm-up” refers to a psychological adjustment to the task, not warming muscles before exercise.

Why warm-up decrement happens

Re-establishing the performer’s “set”

One explanation is that the learner temporarily loses the set that supported skilled performance before the break. Select each card to see what can be part of that set.

Why this is not simply forgetting

Activities that restore the task set can reduce warm-up decrement even though they do not restore memory for the task itself. Activities that disrupt the set can make the decrement larger.

Apply it

A basketball player has not shot for 20 minutes. The first free throw is poor, but after two or three practice shots performance returns to the previous level. What is the best interpretation?

A practical implication

Pre-shot routines may help partly because they restore the attentional, perceptual, and postural set needed for the upcoming action — reducing the warm-up decrement before the “real” attempt.

Wrap up

What you should take from retention

  • 1The retention interval is the period between practice and a later retention test; longer intervals can reveal retention loss.
  • 2Retention is strongly influenced by the type of task: the discrete, memory-heavy switch–light task showed substantial forgetting, while continuous tracking remained durable even after years.
  • 3Warm-up decrement is a temporary drop at the beginning of performance after a break and should not automatically be interpreted as forgetting.
  • 4Reinstating the performer’s set can help restore performance quickly.
